# Failure Pathway and Progression

Nodes 3 through 6 of the CLE event tree depend on what the eroding flaw meets on its way through the embankment: whether a filter intercepts it,
which soils must hold a roof over the enlarging pipe, whether material from upstream can fill it, and whether an upstream element restricts the
flow into it. This chapter documents the failure-pathway conventions that decide those questions and how each of the four nodes is evaluated.

## The Failure Pathway

The failure pathway is the route the concentrated leak takes through the embankment, from the upstream face to the downstream face. Its form follows
from the flaw:

- **Crest-crack models** — the pathway runs along the transverse crack, which is open over a range of elevations from its bottom $z_{bc}$ up to the
  crest. The first drain or filter zone the pathway meets defines the top-of-filter elevation $z_{tf}$. While the pool is at or below $z_{tf}$, the
  water entering the crack reaches the filter: this is the **below-filter pathway**, from the upstream face to the filter, taken at the
  bottom-of-crack elevation. Once the pool rises above $z_{tf}$, the water entering the upper part of the crack passes over the filter's top: this
  is the **full pathway**, from the upstream face to the downstream face. A filter **caps the pathway** when its top reaches the crest and no
  full-pathway materials are given: the below-filter pathway then governs at every pool level. If full-pathway materials are supplied, pools
  above the filter's top are evaluated on the full pathway. With no filter on the pathway, the full pathway governs at every pool level.
- **Compaction models** — the pathway runs through the embankment section at the base-of-flaw elevation $z_f$. The flaw, and so the pathway, is at
  one elevation: whether a filter intercepts it, which materials it passes through, and which walls reach it are the same at every pool level.

The user resolves the pathway against the embankment section and supplies the result — the top-of-filter elevation, the filter and base-soil
description, and the material lists — as documented in [Common Input Parameters](./03-input-parameters.mdx#failure-pathway-inputs).

## Node 3: Continuation

*Question:* Does an unfiltered seepage exit exist that allows erosion to continue?

Where a filter intercepts the pathway, the probability that erosion continues past it, $P_{ce}$, is evaluated by the shared continuation method
from the filter and base-soil description. The method, its alternative evaluation routes, and its basis are documented in the Continuation (Filter
Evaluation) Screening Technical Reference Manual; it draws on the toolbox methods of the
[Filter Evaluation (Continuation)](/docs/toolbox-technical-manuals/internal-erosion-suite/filter-evaluation-continuation/v1.0/general-overview)
manual. Where no filter intercepts the pathway, the exit is unfiltered and continuation is certain.

**Crest-crack models.** Continuation follows the pathway that governs at each pool level:

<Equation
  equationKey="cle-common-screening-eq-13"
  equation="P_{cont}(h) = \begin{cases} P_{ce} & \text{a filter is present and } \bigl(h \le z_{tf} \text{ or the filter caps the pathway}\bigr) \\[4pt] 1.0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}"
/>

Above the top of a filter that does not cap the pathway, the water in the upper part of the crack passes over the filter and exits unfiltered, so
continuation is certain there. A crest-crack SRP curve with a filter therefore steps where the pool crosses $z_{tf}$: continuation rises from
$P_{ce}$ to 1.0, but roof support switches at the same level from the below-filter minimum to the full-pathway minimum, which can be much lower
(for example 0.01 for a downstream rockfill shell classified as GP on the full pathway). The SRP steps up where $P_{ce}$ is smaller than the ratio of the
full-pathway to the below-filter roof support, and steps down where it is larger (see
[Node 4: Progression — Roof Support](#node-4-progression--roof-support)).

**Compaction models.** Continuation is one value for every pool level: $P_{ce}$ when a filter intercepts the pathway at the flaw elevation, and
1.0 when none does. The continuation method is not evaluated when no filter is present.

:::warning[INPUT NEEDED IN-12 — Engineering Basis]
The crest-crack pathway convention requires an engineering basis to state here: (1) continuation and roof support share one gate at the top of the
first filter the pathway meets, so that above that elevation erosion is taken to exit unfiltered over the filter's top with certain continuation;
(2) the below-filter roof materials are taken at the bottom-of-crack elevation; and (3) a filter whose top reaches the crest, with no full-pathway
materials given, caps the pathway at every pool level. Provide the literature source or the engineering-judgment attribution.

Proposed (unconfirmed): a crest crack is open over its full depth, so a pool above the filter's top drives flow through the upper part of the
crack over the filter, where nothing filters the exit; below the filter's top, all flow through the crack reaches the filter. Confirm or replace.
:::

## Node 4: Progression — Roof Support

*Question:* Does a continuous stable roof form over the enlarging pipe, so that it stays open?

Each soil classification on the pathway maps to a roof-support probability per <TableReference tableKey="cle-common-screening-table-13" />,
following Fell et al. (2024) <Citation citationKey="Fell2024" />. Every material along the pathway must hold a roof for the pipe to progress, so
the least capable material governs and the node takes the minimum across the listed materials:

<Equation equationKey="cle-common-screening-eq-14" equation="P_{roof} = \min_{m \in \mathcal{M}} P_{roof}(m)" />

where $\mathcal{M}$ is the material set that applies:

- **Crest-crack models** — the below-filter materials at pool levels at or below $z_{tf}$ (and at every pool level when the filter caps the
  pathway), and the full-pathway materials above it (and at every pool level when no filter is present). Roof support can therefore change where
  the pool crosses $z_{tf}$, in step with continuation.
- **Compaction models** — the embankment materials the pipe passes through, at every pool level.

## Node 6: Progression — No Flow Limitation

*Question:* Does the flow into the enlarging pipe remain unrestricted by upstream seepage-limiting elements?

Each upstream element maps to a probability of no flow limitation per <TableReference tableKey="cle-common-screening-table-15" />, following Fell
et al. (2024) <Citation citationKey="Fell2024" />. When more than one element applies, the most effective one controls the flow, so the node takes
the minimum across the applicable elements; with no applicable element, flow is not limited and $P_{nl} = 1.0$:

<Equation equationKey="cle-common-screening-eq-15" equation="P_{nl} = \min_{e \in \mathcal{E}} P_{nl}(e), \qquad P_{nl} = 1.0 \text{ when } \mathcal{E} \text{ is empty}" />

### Walls that stop below the crest

A wall limits flow only over its own height. When the user supplies the top-of-wall elevation $z_{tw}$, the walls apply only where the leak
passes beneath their top, while the upstream facing element, which is not a wall, applies wherever it is present. The applicable set $\mathcal{E}$
is therefore:

- **Crest-crack models** — gated per pool level. At pool levels at or below $z_{tw}$ every listed element applies; above it the walls drop out
  and only the upstream facing element remains, because the flow entering the crack above the wall's top passes over it.
- **Compaction models** — gated once, against the flaw elevation. When $z_{tw} \ge z_f$ the wall reaches the flaw and every listed element applies;
  when $z_{tw} < z_f$ the walls drop out and only the upstream facing element remains. Either way, one value applies at every pool level.

With no top-of-wall elevation, the walls are taken to reach the crest and every listed element applies at every pool level.

The conduit compaction model carries no flow-limitation input: it models no upstream element, and its Node 6 is fixed at 1.0.

:::warning[INPUT NEEDED IN-14 — Engineering Basis]
The flow-limitation conventions require an engineering basis to state here: (1) the minimum across several upstream elements governs; (2) a wall
stops limiting flow above its top — per pool level for a crest crack, and against the base of the flaw for a compaction flaw — while an upstream
facing element applies at every pool level; and (3) the conduit compaction model takes no flow-limitation credit at all. Provide the literature
source (including the table in Fell et al. (2024) that gives the values of
<TableReference tableKey="cle-common-screening-table-15" />) or the engineering-judgment attribution.
:::
