# Failure Pathway and Progression

Nodes 3 through 6 of the concentrated leak erosion (CLE) event tree depend on what the eroding flaw meets on its way through the embankment: whether a filter intercepts it,
which soils must hold a roof over the enlarging pipe, whether material from upstream can fill it, and whether an upstream element restricts the
flow into it. This chapter documents the failure-pathway conventions that decide those questions and how each of the four nodes is evaluated.

## The Failure Pathway

The failure pathway is the route the concentrated leak takes through the embankment, from the upstream face to the downstream face. Its form follows
from the flaw:

- **Crest-crack models** — the pathway runs along the transverse crack, which is open over a range of elevations from its bottom $z_{bc}$ up to the
  crest. The first drain or filter zone the pathway meets defines the top-of-filter elevation $z_{tf}$. While the pool is at or below $z_{tf}$, the
  water entering the crack reaches the filter: this is the **below-filter pathway**, from the upstream face to the filter, taken at the
  bottom-of-crack elevation. Once the pool rises above $z_{tf}$, the water entering the upper part of the crack passes over the filter's top: this
  is the **full pathway**, from the upstream face to the downstream face. A filter **caps the pathway** when its top reaches the crest and no
  full-pathway materials are given: the below-filter pathway then governs at every pool level. If full-pathway materials are supplied, pools
  above the filter's top are evaluated on the full pathway. With no filter on the pathway, the full pathway governs at every pool level.
- **Compaction models** — the pathway runs through the embankment section at the base-of-flaw elevation $z_f$. The flaw, and so the pathway, is at
  one elevation: whether a filter intercepts it, which materials it passes through, and which walls reach it are the same at every pool level.

The user resolves the pathway against the embankment section and supplies the result — the top-of-filter elevation, the filter and base-soil
description, and the material lists — as documented in [Input Parameters](./03-input-parameters.mdx#failure-pathway-inputs).

## Node 3: Continuation

*Question:* Does an unfiltered seepage exit exist that allows erosion to continue?

Once initiated, erosion continues unless the eroding forces are reduced or the passage of the eroded particles is impeded. Continuation therefore
turns mainly on the filter compatibility of the zones and layers that the eroded core soil meets. In a modern embankment, filters prevent the
migration of fines between zones and protect against leakage through cracks. In an existing embankment without modern filters, continuation
depends on the compatibility of the core with the transition or shell materials downstream of it; where several zones grade from finer to
coarser, each zone must be filter-compatible with the one before it, so the evaluation can be a multi-step process.

Where a filter intercepts the pathway, the probability that erosion continues past it, $P_{ce}$, is evaluated by the shared continuation method
from the filter and base-soil description. The method, its alternative evaluation routes, and its basis are documented in the Continuation (Filter
Evaluation) Screening manual; it draws on the toolbox methods of the
[Filter Evaluation (Continuation)](/docs/toolbox-technical-manuals/internal-erosion-suite/filter-evaluation-continuation/v1.0/general-overview)
manual. $P_{ce}$ is the node probability that the Continuation (Filter Evaluation) Screening manual writes $P_{cont}$; it is not that
manual's Foster and Fell probability of the continuing-erosion condition, $P_{CE}$. Where no filter intercepts the pathway, the exit is
unfiltered and continuation is certain.

**Crest-crack models.** Continuation follows the pathway that governs at each pool level:

<Equation
  equationKey="cle-common-methods-screening-eq-14"
  equation="P_{cont}(h) = \begin{cases} P_{ce} & \text{a filter is present and } \bigl(h \le z_{tf} \text{ or the filter caps the pathway}\bigr) \\[4pt] 1.0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}"
/>

where $P_{ce}$ is the probability of continuing erosion from the continuation method and $z_{tf}$ is the top-of-filter elevation.

Above the top of a filter that does not cap the pathway, the water in the upper part of the crack passes over the filter and exits unfiltered, so
continuation is certain there. A crest-crack SRP curve with a filter therefore steps where the pool crosses $z_{tf}$: continuation rises from
$P_{ce}$ to 1.0, but roof support switches at the same level from the below-filter minimum to the full-pathway minimum, which can be much lower
(for example 0.01 for a downstream rockfill shell classified as GP on the full pathway). The SRP steps up where $P_{ce}$ is smaller than the ratio of the
full-pathway to the below-filter roof support, and steps down where it is larger (see
[Node 4: Progression — Roof Support](#node-4-progression--roof-support)).

**Compaction models.** Continuation is one value for every pool level: $P_{ce}$ when a filter intercepts the pathway at the flaw elevation, and
1.0 when none does. The continuation method is not evaluated when no filter is present.

first filter the pathway meets, so that above that elevation erosion is taken to exit unfiltered over the filter's top with certain continuation;
(2) the below-filter roof materials are taken at the bottom-of-crack elevation; and (3) a filter whose top reaches the crest, with no full-pathway
materials given, caps the pathway at every pool level. Provide the literature source or the engineering-judgment attribution.

## Node 4: Progression — Roof Support

*Question:* Does a continuous stable roof form over the enlarging pipe, so that it stays open?

Two conditions chiefly decide whether soil can hold a stable roof and sidewalls over a pipe through the embankment: a fines content of at least
15 percent, whatever the plasticity of the fines, or an embedded structure — a conduit, a spillway wall, or another feature — that can support a
roof. An embankment core usually satisfies the first, since it typically consists of impervious or semi-impervious soil with sufficient fines.
Table 13.1 of Fell et al. (2024) <Citation citationKey="Fell2024" /> gives guidance on a soil's ability to hold a roof based on its
classification, fines content, plasticity of fines, and moisture condition; the screening models simplify that guidance to the soil
classification alone.

Each soil classification on the pathway maps to a roof-support probability per <TableReference tableKey="cle-common-methods-screening-table-13" />,
following Table 13.1 of Fell et al. (2024) <Citation citationKey="Fell2024" />. Every material along the pathway must hold a roof for the pipe to
progress, so the least capable material governs and the node takes the minimum across the listed materials:

<Equation equationKey="cle-common-methods-screening-eq-15" equation="P_{roof} = \min_{m \in \mathcal{M}} P_{roof}(m)" />

where $\mathcal{M}$ is the material set that applies:

- **Crest-crack models** — the below-filter materials at pool levels at or below $z_{tf}$ (and at every pool level when the filter caps the
  pathway), and the full-pathway materials above it (and at every pool level when no filter is present). Roof support can therefore change where
  the pool crosses $z_{tf}$, in step with continuation.
- **Compaction models** — the embankment materials the pipe passes through, at every pool level.

The node takes no credit for swelling of the soil closing the crack and stopping erosion, which is conservative.

## Node 5: Progression — No Crack Filling

*Question:* Does the enlarging pipe remain unfilled, rather than being filled and healed by material carried in from upstream?

Crack filling requires a granular zone upstream of the core whose particles are small enough to be carried by the water flowing into the crack or
pipe, and a downstream filter, transition, or rockfill zone able to act as a filter for those particles and for the core. When the pipe has
progressed through the core, an upstream zone that cannot itself hold a roof can collapse into it, and the washed-in particles then build a filter
against the downstream zone. The washed-in material is most useful where the core lacks the sand-sized particles needed for it to be filter
compatible with the downstream zone on its own. Crack filling is therefore possible only in central-core and sloping-core earth and rockfill
embankments, and its effectiveness depends on the compatibility of the upstream granular material with the downstream filter or transition, and
of that zone, with the washed-in particles, with the core.

The probability $P_{nf}$ that crack filling does not stop the erosion depends only on the embankment type, per
<TableReference tableKey="cle-common-methods-screening-table-14" />, following Table 13.2 of Fell et al. (2024) <Citation citationKey="Fell2024" />.
Homogeneous and zoned earthfill embankments have no upstream zone able to fill the pipe, so no credit is taken and $P_{nf} = 1.0$; a central-core
earth and rockfill embankment is credited with a one-in-two chance that crack filling arrests the erosion, so $P_{nf} = 0.5$. The node is the
same at every pool level for both groups.

## Node 6: Progression — No Flow Limitation

*Question:* Does the flow into the enlarging pipe remain unrestricted by upstream seepage-limiting elements?

A constriction along the leakage path can limit the flow from the reservoir enough to keep the hydraulic shear stresses below the values needed
to detach soil, arresting erosion before it progresses to breach. The constriction must itself be stable and have openings small enough to limit
the flow. Examples are concrete or sheet pile walls within the embankment — a modern concrete wall is the most effective, while a steel sheet pile
wall may be less effective where driving conditions or construction were poor — and concrete or steel membranes, soil-cement slope protection,
geomembranes, or other linings on the upstream face.

Each upstream element maps to a probability of no flow limitation per <TableReference tableKey="cle-common-methods-screening-table-15" />, following Table
13.3 of Fell et al. (2024) <Citation citationKey="Fell2024" />. The upstream facing elements — a concrete slab, soil-cement wave protection, a
geomembrane, or another lining — share one probability, so the kind of facing is not distinguished. An upstream facing element and a wall
are separate barriers that the flow passes in turn, so when both apply the node is the product of the facing element's probability and the
wall's. When more than one wall type is listed, the walls describe the single barrier located by the top-of-wall elevation, so the most effective
wall governs rather than the walls compounding. An element that is absent, or that does not apply, contributes 1.0: with one applicable element
the node is that element's probability, and with none, flow is not limited and $P_{nl} = 1.0$:

<Equation equationKey="cle-common-methods-screening-eq-16" equation="P_{nl} = P_{nl,f} \times \min_{w \in \mathcal{W}} P_{nl}(w), \qquad P_{nl,f} = 1.0 \text{ with no facing element}, \qquad \min_{w \in \mathcal{W}} P_{nl}(w) = 1.0 \text{ when } \mathcal{W} \text{ is empty}" />

where:

> $P_{nl,f}$ = probability of no flow limitation of the upstream facing element  
> $\mathcal{W}$ = set of walls that apply  
> $P_{nl}(w)$ = probability of no flow limitation of wall $w$

### Walls That Stop Below the Crest

A wall limits flow only over its own height. When the user supplies the top-of-wall elevation $z_{tw}$, the walls apply only where the leak
passes beneath their top, while the upstream facing element, which is not a wall, applies wherever it is present. The set of walls
$\mathcal{W}$ that apply is therefore:

- **Crest-crack models** — gated per pool level. At pool levels at or below $z_{tw}$ the walls apply, and the node is the facing element's
  probability times the governing wall's; above it the walls drop out and only the upstream facing element remains, because the flow entering the
  crack above the wall's top passes over it.
- **Compaction models** — gated once, against the flaw elevation. When $z_{tw} \ge z_f$ the wall reaches the flaw and the walls apply; when
  $z_{tw} < z_f$ the walls drop out and only the upstream facing element remains. Either way, one value applies at every pool level.

With no top-of-wall elevation, the walls are taken to reach the crest and apply at every pool level.

For example, consider a crest crack in a dam whose crest is at elevation 265 ft, with an upstream facing element and a sheet pile wall whose top
is at elevation 240 ft, each with a probability of no flow limitation of 0.5. At pool levels at or below 240 ft both barriers apply, and
$P_{nl} = 0.5 \times 0.5 = 0.25$. Above 240 ft the flow passes over the wall, only the facing element applies, and $P_{nl} = 0.5$. Had a modern
cutoff wall been listed with the sheet pile wall, the modern cutoff wall would govern the walls, giving $P_{nl} = 0.5 \times 0.001 = 0.0005$ at or
below 240 ft and 0.5 above it.

The conduit compaction model carries no flow-limitation input: it models no upstream element, and its Node 6 is fixed at 1.0.
