# Flaw Conventions

Node 1 of every CLE screening model is the probability $P_{flaw}$ that a continuous flaw of the model's mechanism exists through the embankment.
Each model's own manual documents its factors, its category and banding tables, and its flaw-probability curve. This chapter documents the
conventions those assessments share: the risk-factor and likelihood-factor scoring scheme, the optional flaw-continuity stage, the observation
overrides, the treatment of an inapplicable mechanism, and the flaw elevation each assessment passes to the downstream nodes.

## Risk-Factor and Likelihood-Factor Scoring

Seven of the eight models — every model except cross-valley differential settlement, which uses a settlement-based relationship documented in its
own manual — estimate the flaw probability by the risk-factor and likelihood-factor scoring of Fell et al. (2024) <Citation citationKey="Fell2024" />.
The scheme turns a small number of site factors, each judged on a common scale, into one flaw probability.

### Factors

Each model defines its own list of factors. Every factor $j$ carries two numbers:

- **Risk factor** $RF_j$ — a small integer weight expressing how strongly the factor influences the likelihood of a flaw. Six of the seven
  scoring models weight three factors 3, 2, and 1; the conduit compaction model weights four factors 3, 2, 2, and 2.
- **Likelihood factor** $LF_j$ — the factor's score for the site, on a scale on which 1 is the least likely condition for a flaw and 4 the most
  likely. A factor's likelihood factor comes either from a category the user selects (for example a compaction category or a climate) or from a
  banding rule that places a measured quantity (for example a width-to-height ratio or a settlement ratio) into one of the bands. Some categories
  — typically Unknown, or a condition between two bands — carry intermediate scores such as 2.5 or 3.5.

### Weighted sum

The scores combine into the weighted sum

<Equation equationKey="cle-common-screening-eq-2" equation="\Sigma = \sum_{j} RF_j \cdot LF_j" />

which rises as more of the factors, and the more heavily weighted factors, point toward a flaw.

### Flaw probability from the weighted sum

The weighted sum is converted to a flaw probability by a curve tabulated at a few points $(\Sigma_k, P_k)$ for each model. Between two tabulated
points the logarithm of the probability is interpolated linearly in the weighted sum:

<Equation
  equationKey="cle-common-screening-eq-3"
  equation="\log_{10} P_{raw}(\Sigma) = \log_{10} P_k + \frac{\Sigma - \Sigma_k}{\Sigma_{k+1} - \Sigma_k}\,\bigl(\log_{10} P_{k+1} - \log_{10} P_k\bigr), \qquad \Sigma_k \le \Sigma \le \Sigma_{k+1}"
/>

so that equal steps in the weighted sum multiply the probability by equal factors. A weighted sum beyond either end of the tabulated range takes
the probability at that end. In several models the lowest tabulated point carries a negligibly small probability, so that the probability falls
steeply toward the low end of the tabulated range.

Two further rules can force the raw flaw probability $P_{raw}$ to zero before the curve is consulted:

- **Incompatible-condition factors** — a factor may be marked as one whose least likely score (a likelihood factor of exactly 1) describes a
  condition physically incompatible with a continuous flaw. When any such factor scores 1, the flaw probability is zero whatever the other factors
  score. The wall or cliff settlement model marks two of its geometric factors this way, and the foundation or abutment compaction model marks its
  foundation-preparation factor.
- **Lower threshold** — a model may set a weighted sum below which the flaw probability is zero. The desiccation and frost-cracking models use a
  threshold of 13: a weighted sum below 13 gives a zero flaw probability, while a sum of exactly 13 takes the first tabulated value.

A flaw probability forced to zero by either rule leaves the SRP at the negligible value described in
[Event Tree](./04-event-tree.mdx#srp-computation), unless an observation override applies: in the desiccation and frost-cracking models a reported
observation sets the flaw probability to 1.0 even when the weighted sum is below the threshold of 13 (see
[Observation Overrides](#observation-overrides)). Only an inapplicable mechanism sets the override aside
(see [Inapplicable Mechanism](#inapplicable-mechanism)).

## Flaw-Continuity Stage

Three compaction models — poor compaction within the core, adjacent to a wall or cliff, and along the foundation or abutment — apply a second
stage after the scoring. The raw flaw probability is multiplied by a flaw-continuity probability $P_{cf}$, interpolated in the same log-linear
manner from the likelihood factor of the model's compaction factor:

<Equation equationKey="cle-common-screening-eq-4" equation="P_{flaw} = P_{raw}(\Sigma) \times P_{cf}(LF_{comp})" />

where $LF_{comp}$ is the likelihood factor of the core-compaction category (core compaction), of the wall-fill compaction category (wall or cliff
compaction), or of the contact-zone compaction method (foundation or abutment compaction). The three models share the flaw-continuity curve of

A fractional compaction score, such as the 3.5 assigned to an Unknown core-compaction category, is interpolated between the tabulated points. The
other four scoring models (wall or cliff settlement, conduit compaction, desiccation, and frost cracking) use the scored probability directly:

is intended for all three models, keyed to a different compaction factor in each.

## Observation Overrides

Most models accept an observation that demonstrates the flaw directly. When the observation is reported, the flaw probability is set to 1.0 whatever
the factors score; in the three compaction models with an override, the factor selections also become optional. The observations are:

- **Cross-valley differential settlement** — continuous transverse cracking observed.
- **Desiccation cracking and frost cracking** — transverse cracking observed and judged continuous.
- **Poor compaction within the core** — embankment leakage or wet spots observed. The observed elevation of the leakage or wet spots also becomes
  the flaw elevation (see [Flaw Elevation](#flaw-elevation)).
- **Poor compaction adjacent to a conduit** — leakage observed adjacent to the conduit. The flaw elevation is unchanged.
- **Poor compaction adjacent to a wall or cliff** — leakage observed adjacent to the wall. The flaw elevation is unchanged.

The wall or cliff settlement and the foundation or abutment compaction models have no observation override. In the foundation or abutment
compaction model, a well-prepared foundation (a foundation-preparation likelihood factor of 1) instead forces the flaw probability to zero, as
described under [incompatible-condition factors](#flaw-probability-from-the-weighted-sum).

The override sets only Node 1. Every downstream node is evaluated as usual, so an observed flaw still needs the pool to reach it, an unfiltered
exit, and the progression conditions before it contributes to the SRP.

## Inapplicable Mechanism

The desiccation and frost-cracking models score the soils the crest crack would pass through. When every listed crest soil is one in which the
mechanism cannot occur (one of the clean or nearly clean sands and gravels SP-SM, SW, SP, GP-GM, GW, and GP, the classes with less than
12 percent fines), the mechanism does not apply: no crack forms, the flaw probability is negligible, the observation
override is not consulted, and initiation is zero at every pool level (see [Initiation](./06-initiation.mdx#crest-crack-models-headwater-gate)).
The soil sets are documented in those models' manuals.

## Flaw Elevation

Each flaw assessment passes one elevation to the downstream nodes along with the flaw probability. It fixes where erosion can begin, where the
failure pathway runs, and which filter and upstream walls the pathway meets.

### Crest-crack models: bottom of the crack

The flaw of a crest-crack model is a transverse crack open at the crest. The flaw assessment estimates the crack depth $d_c$ — from the settlement
and geometry of the mechanism, or from tabulated depths by climate and soil — and the bottom of the crack is

<Equation equationKey="cle-common-screening-eq-5" equation="z_{bc} = z_c - d_c" />

where $z_c$ is the crest elevation. Water can enter the crack only when the pool rises above $z_{bc}$, which is the initiation gate of
[Initiation](./06-initiation.mdx#crest-crack-models-headwater-gate). When an inapplicable mechanism produces no crack, the crack depth is zero and
$z_{bc}$ equals the crest elevation.

### Compaction models: base of the flaw

The flaw of a compaction model is idealized as a cylindrical pipe whose invert is the base-of-flaw elevation $z_f$. The four models resolve it in
three ways:

- **Normal-pool heuristic** — for poor compaction within the core, and for poor compaction along an abutment contact, the base of the flaw is
  placed one third of the way from the normal pool elevation $z_{np}$ to the crest:

  <Equation equationKey="cle-common-screening-eq-6" equation="z_f = z_{np} + \tfrac{1}{3}\,\bigl(z_c - z_{np}\bigr)" />

  The crest must be above the normal pool. For the core model, an observation of leakage or wet spots replaces this with the observed elevation.
- **Direct input** — for poor compaction adjacent to a conduit, the base of the flaw is the bottom-of-conduit elevation; for poor compaction along
  a foundation contact, it is the embankment–foundation contact elevation.
- **Derived from the wall height** — for poor compaction adjacent to a wall or cliff, the base of the flaw is the crest elevation less the wall or
  cliff height $h_w$, $z_f = z_c - h_w$. The model also requires it to lie at or above the lowest segment bottom of the shell section.

In all four models, the base of the flaw may not lie above the top of the embankment section used for initiation (the core section when one is
supplied, otherwise the shell), nor below the lowest segment bottom of either face of that section, and it must be a positive elevation (see
[Units and Datum](./03-input-parameters.mdx#units-and-datum)).

:::warning[INPUT NEEDED IN-05 — Engineering Basis]
The normal-pool heuristic of <EquationReference equationKey="cle-common-screening-eq-6" /> — placing the base of a core or abutment compaction
flaw one third of the way from the normal pool to the crest — requires an engineering basis. Provide the literature source or the
engineering-judgment attribution to state here, including why the normal pool and not the base of the embankment is the lower reference.

Proposed (unconfirmed): a screening convention that places the flaw within the band of pools the embankment experiences, so that initiation is
evaluated above normal pool where loading is frequent. Confirm or replace.
:::

### Section geometry used in scoring

Where a compaction model scores the geometry of the embankment section — the core compaction and the foundation or abutment compaction models band
the base width $W$ and the width-to-height ratio $W/H_s$ — the two dimensions come from the section used by the model (the core section when
supplied, otherwise the shell; see [Common Input Parameters](./03-input-parameters.mdx#embankment-description)):

<Equation equationKey="cle-common-screening-eq-7" equation="H_s = z_t - z_{base}, \qquad W = W_t + S_{us} + S_{ds}" />

where $H_s$ is the height of the section; $z_t$ and $W_t$ are its top elevation and top width — the core top $z_{t,c}$ and core top width
$W_{t,c}$ for the core section, or the crest $z_c$ and shell top width $W_{t,s}$ for the shell; $z_{base}$ is the lowest segment bottom on
either face; and $S_{us}$ and $S_{ds}$ are the horizontal spans of the upstream and downstream faces from the top down to $z_{base}$, each the sum over its segments of the
vertical extent times the segment slope. Both models accept a user-supplied base width and height in place of the derived values; the supplied
values replace them in the scoring only, while the initiation analysis keeps the section geometry.
