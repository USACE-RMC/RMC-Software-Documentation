# Event Tree

The backward erosion piping (BEP) screening model computes the system response probability (SRP) at each headwater level by multiplying
probabilities from a seven-node event tree. The tree follows the structure defined in the
[Typical Event Tree Database](/docs/toolbox-technical-manuals/risk-calculations-suite/typical-event-tree-database/v1.5/Backward-Erosion-Piping)
for the backward erosion piping failure mode; every database node maps one-to-one onto a screening-model node
(<FigReference figKey="backward-erosion-piping-screening-fig-2" />).

## Node 1: Flaw — Continuous Path

*Question:* Does a continuous path of fine to medium, uniform sand exist in the foundation?

The probability $P_{flaw}$ that a continuous path of erodible foundation sand exists is the mean posterior probability from the Bayesian flaw
assessment documented in [Flaw Assessment](./04-flaw-assessment.mdx). The flaw is a geologic condition, so this probability is constant across
all pool levels and is shared by both exit pathways.

## Node 2: Flaw — Unfiltered Exit

*Question:* Does seepage exit unfiltered at the ground surface?

### Toe Pathway

When a confining layer may be present and the toe exit remains beneath it — no berm, or a berm ending within the layer's known downstream
extent — seepage can reach the surface unfiltered at the toe through either of two mechanisms:

- **Pre-existing defect** — a discontinuity that already penetrates the layer (desiccation crack, root channel, animal burrow, or similar).
  The probability that a defect passes completely through the layer is evaluated as a survival function on the layer thickness:

  <Equation equationKey="backward-erosion-piping-screening-eq-13" equation="P_{defect} = \exp\!\left[-\left(\frac{d_{bl}}{t_c}\right)^{k}\,\right]" />

  where $P_{defect}$ is the probability that a pre-existing defect fully penetrates the layer, $d_{bl}$ is the confining-layer thickness (ft),
  $t_c = 3.5$ ft is the characteristic defect penetration depth, and $k = 1.25$ is the dimensionless shape parameter. The expression is the probability that a defect's penetration depth equals or exceeds the layer thickness under a Weibull
  distribution of defect depths — thinner layers are more likely to be fully penetrated. The probability is evaluated for each sampled layer
  thickness and averaged across the Monte Carlo realizations; it does not depend on pool level.

- **Hydraulic blowout** — rupture of the layer when uplift at its base exceeds its buoyant weight. The blowout probability $P_{blowout}(h)$ at
  each pool level is computed by the blanket-theory analysis using the confining layer's saturated unit weight
  (see [Delegated Methods](./07-delegated-methods.mdx)); it increases with the net head across the layer. The probability is evaluated at the
  exit evaluation point — the toe by default, or the berm toe when a berm ends within the layer's known extent — with the net head measured
  against the water standing on the layer there
  (see [Hydraulic Loading and Seepage Paths](./05-hydraulic-loading-and-seepage-paths.mdx#head-beneath-the-confining-layer)).

The two mechanisms are combined as the union of independent pathways, where $P_{ue}(h)$ is the unfiltered-exit probability at
headwater $h$:

<Equation
  equationKey="backward-erosion-piping-screening-eq-14"
  equation="P_{ue}(h) = P_{defect} + \bigl(1 - P_{defect}\bigr)\, P_{blowout}(h)"
/>

Three site conditions override the composition and force $P_{ue} = 1.0$ at every pool level:

1. **No confining layer** — with the layer's presence answered No, nothing filters the exit.
2. **The exit daylights at the berm toe** — a berm that reaches or clears a confirmed layer's supplied downstream extent, or overlies a
   confirmed layer of unknown extent: the berm toe then sits at or beyond the layer's termination, so nothing filters the exit there. For a
   layer of unknown presence, any berm forces this override — a daylighting exit at the berm toe is assumed rather than established. In all
   these cases the berm toe is itself the daylighting exit and no separate daylight pathway is evaluated (see [Governing SRP](#governing-srp)).
3. **Observed seepage through the layer** — sand boils or concentrated seepage have been observed emerging through the confining layer,
   demonstrating that an unfiltered exit already exists. The override applies to any toe exit that remains beneath the layer, with or
   without a berm.

A berm ending within a confirmed layer's known extent triggers none of these overrides: the through-layer composition above is retained, with
the blowout probability evaluated at the berm toe.

:::warning[INPUT NEEDED IN-13 — Engineering Basis]
The Weibull defect-survival parameters — characteristic penetration depth $t_c = 3.5$ ft and shape parameter $k = 1.25$ — require an
engineering basis. These are empirical values with no published-literature provenance recorded. Provide the data source, calibration
rationale, or engineering-judgment attribution to state here.
:::

:::warning[INPUT NEEDED IN-14 — Engineering Basis]
Two judgments in the berm treatment require an engineering basis to state here:

1. No berm is ever credited with filtering the exit: where the berm end remains beneath the layer, the exit resistance is carried entirely
   by the layer's defect and blowout composition (evaluated at the berm toe), and where the berm daylights the exit, the unfiltered
   exit is certain.
2. When the layer's presence is unknown and a berm is present, a daylighting exit at the berm toe is assumed (forcing the unfiltered exit to
   certainty) rather than evaluated.

Proposed (unconfirmed): for (1), seepage and stability berms are not engineered filters, so no filtering credit is taken for any berm; for
(2), with the layer itself unverified, its lateral extent beneath the berm cannot be relied upon, and assuming a daylighting exit at the berm
toe is the conservative screening posture. Confirm or replace.
:::

:::warning[INPUT NEEDED IN-15 — Engineering Basis]
The union formula treats the defect and blowout mechanisms as statistically independent pathways. Provide the engineering basis or judgment
attribution for the independence treatment to state here.
:::

### Daylight Pathway

At the daylight exit the confining layer has terminated; there is nothing to filter the seepage. The unfiltered-exit probability on the
daylight pathway is 1.0 at every pool level. The pathway itself is evaluated only when the daylighting exit is distinct from the toe exit
(see [Governing SRP](#governing-srp)).

## Node 3: Initiation

*Question:* Is the exit gradient sufficient to initiate backward erosion at the unfiltered exit?

### Toe Pathway

Initiation at a confined exit requires the vertical gradient through the layer (or through a defect in it) to fluidize the underlying sand.
The initiation probability $P_{init}(h)$ at each pool level is the blanket-theory heave probability, computed with the foundation sand's
saturated unit weight and the same net head across the layer as the blowout analysis (see [Delegated Methods](./07-delegated-methods.mdx)).
Four site conditions override the computed value and force $P_{init} = 1.0$:

1. **No confining layer** — with no overburden to resist heave, initiation accompanies the unfiltered exit.
2. **A berm overlies a layer of unknown presence, or a confirmed layer of unknown downstream extent** — a daylighting exit is assumed at the
   berm toe, beyond the characterized layer.
3. **The berm reaches or clears a supplied downstream extent** — the berm is at least as long as the layer's extent, so the exit sits at or
   beyond the layer's termination.
4. **The pool reaches the trigger elevation** — sand boils or concentrated seepage have historically activated at or above this pool, demonstrating initiation:

<Equation
  equationKey="backward-erosion-piping-screening-eq-15"
  equation="P_{init}(h) = \begin{cases} 1.0 & h \geq z_{trig} \\[4pt] P_{heave}(h) & h < z_{trig} \end{cases}"
/>

where $z_{trig}$ is the trigger elevation (ft) and $P_{heave}(h)$ is the blanket-theory heave probability.

The second and third conditions are the berm-toe daylighting cases: they force both this node and the unfiltered-exit node to certainty, and
the separate daylight pathway is not evaluated (see [Governing SRP](#governing-srp)).

### Daylight Pathway

At the daylight exit the sand is exposed at the surface with no confining layer to resist heave, so initiation is certain once seepage
leaves the sand there. Seepage leaves the sand only while the pool stands above the exit level; a pool at or below it fills the sand without
flowing out. The initiation probability on the daylight pathway is therefore

<Equation
  equationKey="backward-erosion-piping-screening-eq-16"
  equation="P_{init,day}(h) = \begin{cases} 1.0 & h > \max(h_t,\, z_{day,ref}) \\[4pt] 0 & h \leq \max(h_t,\, z_{day,ref}) \end{cases}"
/>

where $P_{init,day}(h)$ is the initiation probability on the daylight pathway and $h_t$ is the tailwater elevation at that pool level
(ft), where one exists.

## Node 4: Progression — Mechanical Condition

*Question:* Can the material over the developing pipe sustain a stable roof?

A pipe can only progress if the material above it sustains a stable roof. When the confining layer's presence is confirmed (answered Yes), the
layer itself provides the roof and the probability is 1.0. Otherwise, the probability is evaluated from the embankment materials: each USCS
class maps to a roof-support probability per <TableReference tableKey="backward-erosion-piping-screening-table-19" />, following Fell et
al. (2024) <Citation citationKey="Fell2024" />, and the minimum across the listed materials governs — every material along the pipe path must
hold a roof, so the least capable material controls. The probability is constant across pool levels.

In most cases the embankment core, built of impervious or semi-impervious soil with sufficient fines, provides a stable roof over a pipe
developing in the foundation sand (Fell et al., 2008) <Citation citationKey="Fell2008" />, as in the homogeneous embankment of
<FigReference figKey="backward-erosion-piping-screening-fig-3" />(a). Where the pipe passes beneath coarse materials, such as the gravel or rockfill shells of
<FigReference figKey="backward-erosion-piping-screening-fig-3" />(b), the roof cannot be sustained and the pipe collapses toward the surface as a sinkhole; a
single such material along the path is enough to keep the pipe from remaining open, which is why the least capable listed material governs.

## Node 5: Progression — Hydraulic Condition

*Question:* Is the applied gradient sufficient to advance the pipe toward the impounded water?

The probability that the applied gradient is sufficient to advance the pipe from the exit toward the reservoir is the mean probability of
advancement from the Schmertmann analysis, evaluated at each pool level for the pathway's seepage path length
(see [Delegated Methods](./07-delegated-methods.mdx)). The toe pathway uses the path to the embankment or berm toe; the daylight pathway uses
the longer path to the daylight exit. The resulting probability is constrained to be non-decreasing with pool level, reflecting that, for a net
head that rises with pool level, a higher pool cannot make progression less likely (see assumption 15 in
[Assumptions and Limitations](./08-assumptions-and-limitations.mdx)).

## Nodes 6 and 7: Unsuccessful Intervention and Breach

*Questions:* Do detection and intervention fail (Node 6)? Does an uncontrolled release of impounded water occur (Node 7)?

The final two nodes are the input probabilities documented in [Input Parameters](./03-input-parameters.mdx). Both default to
1.0 — no successful intervention and certain breach — a conservative screening posture.

## SRP Computation

The system response probability for each pathway at each pool level is the product of its seven node probabilities:

<Equation
  equationKey="backward-erosion-piping-screening-eq-17"
  equation="\mathrm{SRP}(h) = P_{flaw} \times P_{ue}(h) \times P_{init}(h) \times P_{roof} \times P_{prog}(h) \times P_{int} \times P_{br}"
/>

where $\mathrm{SRP}(h)$ is the system response probability of the pathway at headwater $h$; $P_{flaw}$ is the probability of a continuous
flaw (Node 1); $P_{ue}(h)$ is the probability of an unfiltered exit (Node 2); $P_{init}(h)$ is the probability of initiation (Node 3);
$P_{roof}$ is the probability of roof support (Node 4); $P_{prog}(h)$ is the probability of hydraulic progression (Node 5); $P_{int}$ is the
probability of unsuccessful intervention (Node 6); and $P_{br}$ is the probability of breach (Node 7).

On the daylight pathway, $P_{ue}$ is 1.0, $P_{init}$ follows <EquationReference equationKey="backward-erosion-piping-screening-eq-16" />, and $P_{prog}$
reflects the daylight path.

## Governing SRP

A daylight pathway is evaluated only where the daylighting exit is distinct from the toe exit. When a berm reaches or clears a confirmed
layer's downstream extent, overlies a confirmed layer of unknown extent, or overlies a layer of unknown presence, the berm toe is itself the
daylighting exit: the toe pathway then carries a certain unfiltered exit and certain initiation over the berm-extended path, which are exactly
the daylighting-exit conditions, so a separate daylight pathway would repeat the same exit node for node and is not evaluated. A separately
supplied daylight ground-surface elevation is not used in these cases.

When both pathways are evaluated, the governing SRP at each pool level is the larger of the two pathway values:

<Equation
  equationKey="backward-erosion-piping-screening-eq-18"
  equation="\mathrm{SRP}_{gov}(h) = \max\bigl(\mathrm{SRP}_{toe}(h),\; \mathrm{SRP}_{day}(h)\bigr)"
/>

where $\mathrm{SRP}_{toe}(h)$ and $\mathrm{SRP}_{day}(h)$ are the toe-pathway and daylight-pathway values of
<EquationReference equationKey="backward-erosion-piping-screening-eq-17" />, and $\mathrm{SRP}_{gov}(h)$ is the governing SRP, which forms the system response curve.

When only the toe pathway exists — no confining layer, or a berm-toe daylighting exit — it governs directly:
$\mathrm{SRP}_{gov}(h) = \mathrm{SRP}_{toe}(h)$.
