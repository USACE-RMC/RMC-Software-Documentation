# Input Parameters

This chapter documents every user input to the BEP screening model. Inputs are organized into eleven groups: hydraulic loading, embankment geometry,
upstream blanket, downstream seepage berm, foundation flaw evidence, confining-layer characterization, observed-seepage refinements, embankment
materials, foundation sand properties, unit weights, and the intervention and breach probabilities. Each group gives the short facts for its
quantities in a table, followed by a description of each input and, for selections, a list of the available choices and what each implies.

Several inputs are statistical distributions that characterize uncertainty in a physical quantity. A distribution input may take any of five
forms: a deterministic (single) value, a uniform distribution between a low and high bound, a normal distribution (mean and standard deviation),
a triangular distribution (low, mode, high), or a PERT distribution (low, mode, high). Any distribution may also carry a lower limit, an upper
limit, or both; a sampled value beyond a limit is set to that limit. The Monte Carlo analyses described in
[Delegated Methods](./07-delegated-methods.mdx) sample these distributions. Geometric seepage-path lengths — the upstream blanket length, the berm
length, and the confining-layer extents — are the exception: a known length is a site measurement entered as a single value. The blanket
length, berm length, and upstream extent are required where they apply. Only the downstream extent may be left blank when it is unknown, and
the model then substitutes the wide default characterization documented in
[Hydraulic Loading and Seepage Paths](./05-hydraulic-loading-and-seepage-paths.mdx).

## Hydraulic Loading

The hydraulic loading is defined by a headwater series and an optional companion tailwater series
(<TableReference tableKey="bep-screening-table-2" />). The model evaluates the event tree at every supplied headwater value and at additional
pool levels distributed between them, with the tailwater at each additional level interpolated linearly between the supplied pairs.

- **Headwater elevation series** — The pool elevations that define the range of hydraulic loading to be evaluated. A headwater value supplied
  more than once is evaluated once, with its first paired tailwater.
- **Tailwater elevation series** — Tailwater elevations paired one-to-one with the headwater series. The entire series may be omitted (no
  tailwater at any pool level), or individual stages may be left blank (no tailwater at that pool level). Wherever no tailwater exists, the
  downstream ground surface controls the net head, as described in
  [Hydraulic Loading and Seepage Paths](./05-hydraulic-loading-and-seepage-paths.mdx).

## Embankment Geometry

The embankment cross-section is described by its crest and by slope-segment tables for the upstream and downstream faces
(<TableReference tableKey="bep-screening-table-3" />). Each face is a sequence of one or more segments ordered from the crest down to the toe;
each segment is defined by its bottom elevation and its slope, expressed as horizontal run per unit of vertical rise (H:V). Multiple segments
accommodate compound slopes and intermediate benches.

- **Crest elevation** $E_c$ — Elevation of the embankment crest.
- **Crest width** $W_c$ — Width of the embankment crest.
- **Upstream face slope segments** — Bottom elevation and slope of each upstream face segment, ordered from the crest to the toe.
- **Downstream face slope segments** — Bottom elevation and slope of each downstream face segment, ordered from the crest to the toe. The
  lowest segment elevation defines the downstream toe, which must lie below the crest.
- **Daylight ground-surface elevation** $E_{day}$ — Ground-surface elevation at the downstream termination of the confining layer, where the
  foundation sand daylights. It may lie above or below the embankment toe. When it is left blank, the daylight exit is assumed to share the toe
  reference elevation (the berm toe when a berm with a specified toe elevation is present, otherwise the downstream embankment toe). It sets the
  exit level that controls the driving head for the blowout, heave, and daylight progression analyses; the blowout and heave net head is still
  measured against the water standing on the layer at the toe or berm toe. When the daylighting exit is the berm toe itself, a supplied value
  is not used, because the berm-toe reference elevation is then the exit datum (see
  [Hydraulic Loading and Seepage Paths](./05-hydraulic-loading-and-seepage-paths.mdx)).

Three geometric quantities are derived from the cross-section rather than entered directly:

- **Downstream toe elevation** $E_{toe}$ — the lowest bottom elevation among the downstream face segments.
- **Embankment base width** $L_2$ — the crest width plus the horizontal projections of the upstream and downstream faces from the crest to
  their respective toes. This is the embankment component of the seepage path. A downstream seepage berm is *not* part of $L_2$; it extends the
  downstream seepage path beyond the toe instead (see [Hydraulic Loading and Seepage Paths](./05-hydraulic-loading-and-seepage-paths.mdx)).
- **Embankment height** $H_d = E_c - E_{toe}$ — used to scale the downstream seepage path when no berm is present and the confining-layer extent or presence is unknown.

## Upstream Blanket

An engineered impervious blanket extending upstream of the embankment lengthens the seepage path and reduces gradients at the downstream exit
(<TableReference tableKey="bep-screening-table-4" />). When an upstream blanket is present, the blowout and heave analyses use Blanket
Theory Case 2, which represents impervious strata on both sides of the embankment (see
[Hydraulic Loading and Seepage Paths](./05-hydraulic-loading-and-seepage-paths.mdx)).

## Downstream Seepage Berm

A downstream seepage or stability berm moves the seepage exit beyond the berm. The model credits no berm weight in the blowout and heave
factors of safety, which depend on the confining-layer thickness alone
(<TableReference tableKey="bep-screening-table-5" />). Its effect on the event tree depends on where the berm ends relative to the confining
layer: a berm ending within a confirmed layer's known extent retains the through-layer exit mechanisms, evaluated at the berm toe, while a
berm that reaches or clears the layer's extent — or overlies a confirmed layer of unknown extent, or a layer of unknown presence — is assumed to produce a daylighting
exit at the berm toe, where the unfiltered exit is certain. Every berm lengthens the toe progression path. The full rules are documented in
[Event Tree](./06-event-tree.mdx) and [Hydraulic Loading and Seepage Paths](./05-hydraulic-loading-and-seepage-paths.mdx).

- **Berm present** (Yes or No) — Whether a downstream seepage or stability berm is present.
- **Berm length** $L_b$ — Length of the berm measured from the downstream embankment toe.

  reference elevation for the net head on the toe progression pathway. When it is left blank, the ground beneath the berm is assumed flat and
  the berm toe shares the downstream embankment toe elevation. It is ignored when no berm is present.

## Foundation Flaw Evidence

The probability that a continuous path of fine to medium, uniform sand exists in the foundation is assessed from three categories of evidence:
the geologic setting, subsurface data, and seepage observations. The way the evidence combines, and the probability range behind each
choice, are documented in [Flaw Assessment](./04-flaw-assessment.mdx).

**Geologic depositional environment** (required) — The depositional setting of the foundation soils. It selects the prior probability range for
the continuous-flaw assessment; environments that deposit laterally continuous, uniform fine sands carry higher priors. The choices are:

- Glacial outwash
- High-energy fluvial systems
- Coastal shoreface and barrier systems
- Aeolian dune systems
- Delta deposits
- Alluvial fans
- Shallow marine shelf
- Low-energy deltaic and coastal mud systems
- Residual soils
- Unknown — the depositional setting has not been established; a moderate, deliberately broad prior applies

**Subsurface data available** (Yes or No) — Whether borings, cone soundings, geophysics, or comparable subsurface information inform the flaw
judgment. When no subsurface data is available, the two selections below are not used.

**Strength of evidence** (required when subsurface data is available) — The degree to which the subsurface data supports or refutes the presence
of a continuous fine sand layer. The choices are:

- Very strong — the data strongly indicates a continuous layer; the flaw probability increases most
- Strong — the data indicates a continuous layer
- Moderate — the data modestly indicates a continuous layer
- Neutral — the data neither supports nor refutes a continuous layer; the flaw probability is unchanged
- Weak — the data tends to indicate that no continuous layer exists; the flaw probability decreases
- Very weak — the data strongly indicates that no continuous layer exists; the flaw probability decreases most

**Subsurface data quality** (required when subsurface data is available) — The quality of the data behind the strength-of-evidence judgment:
extent of coverage, sampling method, and reliability. The choices are:

- High — the evidence receives full weight
- Moderate — the evidence is partly discounted toward neutral
- Low — the evidence is discounted most strongly toward neutral

**Seepage observed** (Yes or No) — Whether seepage has been observed downstream of the embankment during past loading events.

**Seepage observation type** (required when seepage has been observed) — The character of the observed seepage. More localized,
sediment-carrying signatures are stronger evidence of a continuous transmissive flaw than diffuse wet areas. The choices are:

- Sand boils — the strongest seepage evidence of a continuous flaw; also enables the observed-seepage refinements below
- Concentrated seepage — the next strongest evidence; also enables the observed-seepage refinements below
- Stage-dependent wet areas — wet areas that respond to pool level; weaker evidence than concentrated seepage
- Stage-independent wet areas — wet areas that do not respond to pool level; close to uninformative

## Confining-Layer Characterization

The downstream confining layer — a fine-grained stratum overlying the foundation sand on the landside — controls whether seepage can exit
unfiltered at the toe. The presence, the thickness category, the permeability, and the upstream-extension question are each required where they
apply, and Unknown is a valid answer to each.

**Confining layer presence** (required) — Whether a confining layer overlies the foundation sand downstream of the embankment. The choice is
deliberately three-way, so that a layer whose presence cannot be established is neither ignored nor fully credited:

- Yes — the layer is confirmed. The defect and blowout analyses run with the selected thickness category, and the layer itself is credited as
  the roof for pipe progression.
- No — there is no layer. Seepage exits unfiltered at the toe with certainty, and only the toe pathway is evaluated.
- Unknown — the layer may be present. The Unknown thickness band applies (no thickness category is asked), roof support is evaluated from the
  embankment materials, and both exit pathways are evaluated.

**Confining layer thickness category** (required when the presence is Yes) — The qualitative thickness of the layer, chosen by the analyst
from borings or geologic mapping. The choices are:

- Very Thin, Thin, Moderate, or Thick — each maps to a uniform thickness range (<TableReference tableKey="bep-screening-table-8" />)
- Unknown — no thickness information exists; the broad Unknown band applies
- Custom — the user supplies a site-specific thickness distribution, in feet, that cannot produce negative values

**Confining layer permeability** (required when the presence is Yes) — The permeability character of the layer, which decides whether it can
act as an upstream seepage barrier (see [Hydraulic Loading and Seepage Paths](./05-hydraulic-loading-and-seepage-paths.mdx)). The choices
are:

- Impervious — the layer can be credited as an upstream seepage barrier
- Semi-pervious — the layer contributes no upstream head loss
- Unknown — the layer contributes no upstream head loss

**Confining layer extends upstream** (asked when the presence is Yes, the permeability is Impervious, and no upstream blanket is present) —
Whether the confirmed impervious layer continues upstream beneath the reservoir. An engineered upstream blanket takes precedence as the upstream
barrier, so the question is not asked when one is present. The choices are:

- Yes — the layer is treated as the upstream seepage barrier and its upstream length is entered; a Very Thin layer is credited with zero
  upstream length and so uses Blanket Theory Case 4
- No — no upstream head loss is credited
- Unknown — no upstream head loss is credited

The two confining-layer extents are entered as single lengths (<TableReference tableKey="bep-screening-table-6" />).

- **Upstream confining layer length** — Length of the layer beyond the upstream embankment toe. It is required when the layer extends
  upstream. It is credited directly as the upstream seepage path $L_1$, except for a Very Thin layer, which is credited with $L_1 = 0$ (see

- **Downstream confining layer length** $L_{cl}$ — Length of the layer beyond the downstream embankment toe, which is the distance to the
  daylight exit. Leaving it blank means the extent is unknown:
  - With no berm, the downstream seepage path is characterized by a wide default range scaled to the embankment height (see
    [Hydraulic Loading and Seepage Paths](./05-hydraulic-loading-and-seepage-paths.mdx)).

- When a berm is present over a confirmed layer, a supplied extent also decides whether the berm end remains beneath the layer (retaining the
  through-layer exit mechanisms, evaluated at the berm toe) or reaches and clears it (producing a daylighting exit at the berm toe; see
  [Event Tree](./06-event-tree.mdx)).
- As a seepage-path length, a supplied downstream extent is used only when the presence is Yes. When the presence is Unknown, the wide default
  range applies to the no-berm path regardless, and any berm is taken to end the layer at the berm toe.

When sand boils or concentrated seepage have been observed, two additional inputs refine how that observation enters the event tree

- **Seepage through confining layer** (Yes or No) — Whether the observed sand boils or concentrated seepage emerged through the confining layer. Yes

- **Trigger elevation** $E_{trigger}$ — Pool elevation at or above which sand boils or concentrated seepage have historically
  activated (or are expected to activate). At pool levels at or above it, the initiation probability is set to certainty, because initiation has been demonstrated at those
  loads.

## Embankment Materials

**Embankment materials** (required unless the confining layer presence is Yes) — One or more Unified Soil Classification System (USCS)
classes describing the materials overlying the developing pipe along the seepage pathway. Each class maps to a probability that it can sustain a
stable pipe roof, and the minimum across the listed materials governs, because every material along the path must hold a roof for the pipe to
remain open. The mapping is given in [Event Tree](./06-event-tree.mdx). When the confining layer presence is Yes, the layer itself is credited as
the roof and the materials are not used.

## Foundation Sand Properties

The foundation sand — the erodible aquifer beneath the embankment — is characterized by its thickness, relative density, gradation, and fines
content (<TableReference tableKey="bep-screening-table-11" />). The thickness, the relative density category, and the fines content category
are required; Unknown is a valid answer for the two categories. The gradation
properties carry screening defaults that the user may override with site-specific distributions.

The thickness, coefficient of uniformity, and effective grain size are entered as distributions, and the relative density as a category or a
custom distribution. The valid ranges are the physical ranges of each quantity (the coefficient of uniformity cannot be less than 1 by
definition). Apart from the relative density, the model does not test sampled values against these ranges, so a distribution that could stray
outside them should carry lower or upper limits.

- **Foundation sand thickness** $d$ — Thickness of the pervious foundation (aquifer) layer, entered as a distribution. It enters both the
  blanket-theory analysis (the seepage entrance and gradient computations) and the Schmertmann progression analysis (the depth correction).
- **Relative density category** $D_r$ — Qualitative relative density of the foundation sand. Denser sands resist progression through the
  Schmertmann density correction. The choices are:
  - Very Loose, Loose, Medium Dense, Dense, or Very Dense — each maps to a uniform distribution of relative density
    (<TableReference tableKey="bep-screening-table-12" />)
  - Unknown — a broad band spanning loose to medium-dense conditions applies
  - Custom — the user supplies a site-specific distribution; every value it can produce must lie within the physical range of 0 to 100%, so an
    unbounded form such as a normal distribution requires explicit lower and upper limits
- **Coefficient of uniformity** $C_u$ — Coefficient of uniformity of the foundation sand gradation. It sets the base critical gradient in the
  Schmertmann method; more uniform sands (lower values) are more erodible.
- **Effective grain size** $D_{10}$ — Grain size at 10% passing for the foundation sand. It sets the Schmertmann grain-size correction.
- **Fines content category** — Fines content of the foundation sand, which places the sand on one side or the other of the step in the
  Schmertmann fines-content correction (see [Delegated Methods](./07-delegated-methods.mdx)). The choices are:
  - Less than 5% — taken as 0% fines; no fines correction applies
  - 5% or more — taken as 5% fines; the higher fines correction applies
  - Unknown — taken as 0% fines, so no fines credit is taken

## Intervention and Breach Probabilities

The final two event-tree nodes are user-specified probabilities (<TableReference tableKey="bep-screening-table-14" />). Both default to 1.0
(certain), which is conservative for screening.

- **Probability of unsuccessful intervention** $P_{int}$ — Probability that detection and emergency intervention fail to stop the piping
