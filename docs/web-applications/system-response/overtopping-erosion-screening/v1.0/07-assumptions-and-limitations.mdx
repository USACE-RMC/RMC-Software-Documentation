# Assumptions and Limitations

The overtopping erosion screening model applies simplifying assumptions so that a screening can be completed with few inputs. Each assumption below is
stated with its engineering basis, its effect on results and the conditions under which it may not hold. Other chapters refer to these assumptions by
number.

## Embankment and Erosion Mechanism

1. **The embankment is homogeneous with a uniform downstream slope.** A single set of material properties applies to the whole downstream face, and
   no zone boundaries, benches, berms or slope changes are represented.
   - *Engineering basis:* Engineering judgment adopted for screening-level use (attribution pending, IN-16).
   - *Effect on results:* Results reflect one material and one slope; the influence of a core, shells or slope changes on erosion is not captured.
   - *When it may not hold:* Zoned embankments (for example, a clay core with granular shells), embankments with downstream berms or benches, and
     embankments whose slope varies along the downstream face.
2. **Erosion removes material uniformly over the whole downstream face.** The face retreats parallel to itself (surface erosion); headcut formation,
   migration and deepening are not modeled.
   - *Engineering basis:* Engineering judgment adopted for screening-level use (attribution pending, IN-16).
   - *Effect on results:* Spreading erosion over the whole face underestimates the local erosion depth, and the speed at which breach develops, where
     erosion concentrates at a headcut or in localized scour channels. Where that mechanism governs, the model is unconservative: it can understate
     the breach probability.
   - *When it may not hold:* Sites where headcut erosion is the dominant mechanism; a headcut concentrates erosion and can advance through the
     embankment faster than uniform surface erosion.
3. **Breach occurs when the retreating face reaches the upstream edge of the crest.** The critical erosion depth is the perpendicular distance from
   the downstream face to the upstream crest edge, with a minimum of 2.0 ft (0.6 m) ([Chapter 5](./05-erosion-model.mdx)).
   - *Engineering basis:* See IN-13 and IN-14 in [Chapter 5](./05-erosion-model.mdx).
   - *Effect on results:* A wider crest or a steeper downstream slope raises the critical erosion depth, and a flatter slope lowers it; where
     the minimum governs, the geometry has no further effect on the breach threshold.
   - *When it may not hold:* Embankments whose breach develops by another sequence, such as headcut advance through the crest (Assumption 2).

## Hydrograph

4. **Overtopping duration scales with depth from a single reference depth.** The user gives one duration at 0.5 ft (0.15 m), and the model scales it to
   other depths with a fixed exponent of 1/1.8 ([Chapter 4](./04-hydrograph-generation.mdx)).
   - *Engineering basis:* See IN-10 in [Chapter 4](./04-hydrograph-generation.mdx).
   - *Effect on results:* The base duration sets the exposure time at every depth; durations that are too long raise the breach probability and
     durations that are too short lower it.
   - *When it may not hold:* Reservoirs whose duration–depth relationship departs from the power law (see IN-10 in [Chapter 4](./04-hydrograph-generation.mdx)).
5. **Every hydrograph has the same gamma shape.** The shape parameter (default 3.5) is the same at every depth, and the curve has the volume of a
   triangular hydrograph with the scaled duration as its base ([Chapter 4](./04-hydrograph-generation.mdx)).
   - *Engineering basis:* See IN-11 in [Chapter 4](./04-hydrograph-generation.mdx).
   - *Effect on results:* The shape sets how the overflow volume is distributed in time, and so how long the discharge exceeds the grass cover
     threshold and how much erosion the peak produces.
   - *When it may not hold:* Hydrographs with a sudden onset (an upstream dam failure), a sustained plateau (controlled releases) or several peaks.
6. **Overtopping ends at the scaled duration.** The simulation stops at $t_H$, when the discharge is still about 7 percent of the peak with the default
   shape parameter; the remaining tail of the curve is not applied ([Chapter 4](./04-hydrograph-generation.mdx)).
   - *Engineering basis:* See IN-12 in [Chapter 4](./04-hydrograph-generation.mdx).
   - *Effect on results:* Omitting the tail removes about 2 percent of the hydrograph volume with the default shape parameter, which can turn
     realizations close to the breach threshold into non-breaches, lowering the breach probability slightly.
   - *When it may not hold:* Events in which the recession is long and the low flows still exceed the grass cover threshold or the critical shear
     stress.

## Materials and Erosion Rate

7. **Material properties come from the erodibility classification unless the user supplies distributions.** The default erodibility coefficient and
   critical shear stress follow PERT distributions, and the roughness coefficient a Uniform distribution, for the selected classification ([Chapter 3](./03-input-parameters.mdx)).
   - *Engineering basis:* The soil-type categories and the erodibility coefficient and critical shear stress ranges follow published erodibility
     charts and soil testing <Citation citationKey="Briaud2019" /> <Citation citationKey="Hanson2011" /> <Citation citationKey="Wibowo2018" />; the
     roughness ranges are pending (IN-03 in [Chapter 3](./03-input-parameters.mdx)).
   - *Effect on results:* The default distributions span the range of behavior within each category, so the results reflect that whole range rather
     than the site's material.
   - *When it may not hold:* Sites where jet erosion tests or hole erosion tests <Citation citationKey="Wahl2008" /> give properties that differ
     significantly from the category defaults; such results can be supplied as site-specific distributions.
8. **The erosion rate is linear in the excess shear stress.** The default erosion exponent is 1.0 ([Chapter 5](./05-erosion-model.mdx)).
   - *Engineering basis:* The linear excess shear stress equation is the standard formulation in the WinDAM C model framework (USDA
     NRCS) <Citation citationKey="Hanson2011" />.
   - *Effect on results:* With a linear relationship, the erosion rate grows in direct proportion to the shear stress in excess of the critical
     shear stress.
   - *When it may not hold:* Soils whose tested erosion rate does not grow linearly with the excess shear stress; the user can change the exponent,
     but the erodibility coefficient distributions must then be consistent with that exponent, because the default $k_d$ ranges and their units assume
     $\alpha = 1$ ([Chapter 3](./03-input-parameters.mdx)).
9. **Grass cover is the only protection modeled.** The model represents grass cover removal by the CIRIA-based method <Citation citationKey="Hughes2015" /> <Citation citationKey="Thornton2014" />,
   but not riprap stability or removal, or other armoring.
   - *Engineering basis:* Engineering judgment adopted for screening-level use (attribution pending, IN-16).
   - *Effect on results:* The protective effect of riprap or other armoring, which can delay or prevent erosion at significant overtopping depths, is
     not credited, so results for armored embankments can overestimate the breach probability.
   - *When it may not hold:* Embankments with riprap, articulated concrete blocks or other armoring on the downstream face.
10. **The embankment contains no non-erodible elements.** Cutoff walls, sheet pile walls, concrete caps and other features that would stop or redirect
    erosion are not represented.
    - *Engineering basis:* Engineering judgment adopted for screening-level use (attribution pending, IN-16).
    - *Effect on results:* Embankments with such elements may resist breach considerably better than the model predicts.
    - *When it may not hold:* Embankments with structural elements that intersect the erosion path.
11. **The erodibility coefficient and the roughness coefficient are associated only through the critical shear stress.** Their correlation is the
    product of the two specified correlations, 0.12 with the defaults ([Chapter 6](./06-monte-carlo-simulation.mdx)).
    - *Engineering basis:* See IN-15 in [Chapter 6](./06-monte-carlo-simulation.mdx); the default correlation values are pending (IN-09 in [Chapter 3](./03-input-parameters.mdx)).
    - *Effect on results:* With the default values the implied correlation is weak, so its influence on the sampled combinations is small.
    - *When it may not hold:* Sites where measurements show a correlation between the erodibility coefficient and the roughness coefficient that
      differs materially from the implied value.
12. **The flow on the downstream face is at normal depth.** At each time step, the flow depth on the slope is the steady uniform flow depth from
    Manning's equation for the current discharge ([Chapter 5](./05-erosion-model.mdx)).
    - *Engineering basis:* Engineering judgment adopted for screening-level use (attribution pending, IN-16).
    - *Effect on results:* The applied shear stress follows the discharge directly, without the development length or acceleration of flow down the
      face.
    - *When it may not hold:* Short or steep downstream faces on which the flow does not reach normal depth.
13. **The erosion phase uses the slope gradient in place of the sine of the slope angle.** Manning's equation and the applied shear stress use
    $S = 1/Z = \tan\theta$, while the grass cover phase uses $\sin\theta$ ([Chapter 5](./05-erosion-model.mdx)).
    - *Engineering basis:* Pending. Whether the use of the gradient in the erosion phase is intended, and its basis, are open questions (IN-16).
    - *Effect on results:* $\tan\theta$ exceeds $\sin\theta$, so the applied shear stress is higher than with the sine. For a 3H:1V slope the gradient
      is about 5 percent larger than the sine, which raises the applied shear stress by about 4 percent; the difference grows as the slope steepens.
    - *When it may not hold:* Steep downstream faces, where the gradient and the sine of the slope angle differ materially.
14. **Each realization uses the same material properties at every overtopping depth.** One sample of $k_d$, $\tau_c$ and $n$ is drawn per
    realization and evaluated at all depths on the curve ([Chapter 6](./06-monte-carlo-simulation.mdx)).
    - *Engineering basis:* Engineering judgment adopted for screening-level use (attribution pending, IN-16).
    - *Effect on results:* Reusing the samples does not change the breach probability at any single depth, which depends only on the distribution of
      the properties. It makes the curve consistent from depth to depth: differences between depths come from the hydrographs alone, not from
      resampling the material.

## Duration Scaling

The model takes a single duration at the reference depth and extrapolates every other duration with a fixed exponent (Assumption 4). The scaled
durations govern both phases of the erosion model. Grass cover removal is a threshold on the cumulative excess overflow volume, which grows with the
duration, so the duration controls whether the cover is removed at all. Once the cover is removed and the applied shear stress exceeds the critical
shear stress, the erosion depth grows with the time the flow lasts, so an error in the scaled duration carries directly into the erosion depth. The
duration has little effect on erosion only where the applied shear stress stays below the critical shear stress. How overtopping duration grows
with depth differs from reservoir to reservoir (see IN-10 in [Chapter 4](./04-hydrograph-generation.mdx)). The engineer should check that the scaled durations across the depth range are
plausible for the reservoir and adjust the base duration if they are not.

## Uniform Erosion

The uniform erosion assumption (Assumption 2) is a fundamental simplification. Overtopping erosion of embankment dams often proceeds by headcut
formation and migration: a steep scarp forms on the downstream face and advances upstream through the embankment (source pending, IN-22). Detailed software such as WinDAM C
(USDA NRCS) models the headcut mechanism; this screening model does not, and its breach timing can therefore differ from that of a headcut model.

The screening results have been compared with Quantitative Risk Assessment (QRA) response functions from USACE dam safety studies to confirm that they
are reasonable relative to detailed assessments ([Chapter 8](./08-output-and-interpretation.mdx) lists the studies).

## Scope Exclusions

The following are outside the scope of the screening model:

- **Rockfill embankments** — the erodibility classifications and erosion equations apply to earth embankments; rockfill erodes by different
  mechanisms.
- **Riprap removal** — the stability and progressive removal of riprap armoring are not modeled (Assumption 9).
- **Headcut erosion** — the model uses surface erosion and does not model the headcut mechanism (Assumption 2).
- **Zoned embankments** — internal zoning, filters and drains are not represented (Assumption 1).
- **Non-erodible elements** — cutoff walls, sheet pile, concrete caps and other features that would stop erosion are not represented
  (Assumption 10).
